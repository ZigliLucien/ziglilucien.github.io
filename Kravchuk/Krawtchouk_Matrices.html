Krawtchouk Matrices

Kravchuk matrices? You have seen them since your algebra classes in high school.

The reader is asked not to feel offended if we start from such a personal experience as his/her first intimate encounter with the charm of algebra concentrated around ``multiplication rules.'' Perhaps the first vibes came at (a ± b) 2 =a 2 ± 2ab+ b 2 . Let us recall the first amazement caused by vanishing middle terms in the product (a+b)(a-b). These memories of the initiation to the mysteries of mathematics are recalled now, for they are going to be developed on a deeper level and are basic for our main subject. The three rules

(a+b) 2 =a 2 +2ab+b 2
(a+b)(a-b)=a 2 -b 2
(a-b) 2 =a 2 -2ab+b 2

can be coded into a single mnemotechnic matrix:

1 2 1
1 0 -1
1 -2 1

Believe it or not, we are looking at Kravchuk polynomials! Before we show it, let us write the coefficients as columns and define a ``second-order Krawtchouk matrix'' as:

2 K =

1 1 1
2 0 -2
1 -1 1

Similarly, the algebraic rules of degree three translate into a ``third-order Krawtchouk matrix'':

(a+b) 3 = a 3 +3a 2 b+3ab 2 +b 3
(a+b) 2 (a-b) = a 3 +a 2 b-ab 2 -b 3
(a+b)(a-b) 2 = a 3 -a 2 b-ab 2 +b 3
(a-b) 3 = a 3 -3a 2 b+3ab 2 +b 3

are encoded in the matrix

3 K =

1 1 1 1
3 1 -1 -3
3 -1 -1 3
1 -1 1 -1

Similarly, for order 4 one gets:

4 K =

1 1 1 1 1
4 2 0 -2 -4
6 0 -2 0 6
4 -2 0 2 -4
1 -1 1 -1 1

You may also include Krawtchouk matrices of order one and zero:

0 K=

1

1 K=

1 1
1 -1

Thus, rewriting the algebraic rules and normalizing to a=1, b=v, one gets the general definition of Krawtchouk matrices:

Definition
The Nth-order Krawtchouk matrix N K is an (N+1)×(N+1) matrix, the nj entry defined by:

(1+v) i (1-v) j = N
Sum
n=0 		N K nj v n

where N=i+j, and where the polynomial on the left-hand side

G(v)= (1+v) i (1-v) j

is viewed as the generating function for N K.

To recover the algebraic rules, one can use matrix multiplication
vK where v = [1,v,v 2 , . . . ,v N ].

Krawtchouk matrices have numerous beautiful symmetry properties and satisfy many algebraic identities. One remarkable identity (to be proven later) concerns the entries of a single matrix:
in any (sub)square of four adjacent matrix entries, the three upper right numbers sum to the fourth, the entry in the bottom left corner. This is called a ``square identity''.

1 1 1 1 1
4 2 0 -2 -4
6 0 -2 0 6
4 -2 0 2 -4
1 -1 1 -1 1

Test the square identity: In any square of four cells, the left-bottom value is the sum of the other three numbers.

Problems.

  • Notice that the nth row of the matrix N-1 K may be recovered from the (n-1) st row of the succeeding matrix N K by pairwise subtraction.

  • Averaging entries two-by-two along a row recovers the row of the previous matrix.

  • Subtracting a column of N K from its own copy shifted up by one place reconstructs the next column over of the next Krawtchouk matrix, N+1 K. Explain this, starting from

    (a+b) i (a-b) j+1 = (a-b) × (a+b) i (a-b) j .

    Write this in matrix terms.

Problem. Pascal's triangle is clearly valid for the leftmost column, j=0. Does it generalize for the other columns?

Problem. Find a substitute for Pascal's triangle for rows.

Exercise. Calculate the matrix powers $K m $ for integer m > 0.
